Methodology

How every fact here gets checked.

Two different checks live on this site. Approved-medicine facts are checked against the FDA's own records. A smaller, separate check still verifies grey-market vendor certificates - evidence for why the legal routes are worth it, not the site's main argument anymore.

FDA approvals and the pipeline

PeptideChecker runs no laboratory and commissions no testing of any medicine or compound. Every approval on this site is checked against the FDA’s own Drugs@FDA record under the application number shown on the page — the same number anyone can query directly at api.fda.gov. Every pipeline entry is sourced to the company’s own filing or trial announcement, not a reseller’s claim or a copied list. Both pages show the date we last checked them, and both are re-checked monthly.

Cost

The cost page compares insurance, manufacturer direct-pay and licensed telehealth using prices published on each provider’s own site, with the date we read them. We do not estimate or average a price we have not seen published.

How this site makes money

We intend to earn a commission when a reader signs up with a licensed telehealth provider compared on the get-started page. That is not live yet: no provider currently pays us anything, and no affiliate agreement is in place. Every provider we know of is compared the same way regardless.

When it starts, three rules keep the commission away from the comparison. It will never move a price we publish — those come from each provider’s own page, not from us. It will never buy a place in the order providers appear. And a provider paying a higher rate will not read more favourably than one paying nothing: the comparison covers what each one prescribes, whether it is branded or compounded, and how quickly someone is seen, not who pays best.

We earn nothing from the grey-market vendors described below. No vendor listing, on this site or anywhere else, has ever carried a purchase or affiliate link.

What we verify

Seven gates. A certificate clears all seven, or it clears none.

Every listing runs the same sequence in the same order. Each gate asks one question the document either answers or does not, and the run stops at the first no.

Pass condition

7 of 7. No partial credit, no averaging, no score. A failure at any gate ends the check for the whole listing.

  1. 01

    It exists and it loads

    We open the certificate ourselves, from the same public link a buyer would use. A dead URL, a login wall, a file that will not render, or a promise that a COA is available on request all read identically from the outside: there is nothing to check.

    Fails on404 · login wall · “on request”

  2. 02

    It names the laboratory

    A result has to come from somewhere accountable. If the document does not identify the lab that produced it — a name that can be looked up, not a cropped header or an unbranded chart — then it is an assertion with a chart on it.

    Fails onlab: not stated · header cropped

  3. 03

    The lab's own records confirm it

    A lab name on a PDF can be copied onto a forged document. So we check the certificate against the laboratory itself: its public lookup, verification key or QR code has to resolve to this report, with the same compound and result. If the lab cannot confirm the document, nothing proves it issued it.

    Fails onkey: no match · lab denies issuing

  4. 04

    It was issued to this vendor

    Every certificate names the client that sent the sample. If that line names a different company, the document describes someone else's product, borrowed to make a storefront look tested. The client on the certificate has to be the vendor selling the vial.

    Fails onclient: another brand

  5. 05

    It names a specific lot

    A certificate with no lot number cannot be tied to anything. One generic PDF reused across a catalogue describes a batch that was tested once, somewhere, and says nothing about the vial being shipped to you. The lot on the document has to be the lot on the label.

    Fails onlot: absent · one COA, many listings

  6. 06

    It matches the product page

    This gate is arithmetic, not chemistry. Compound and strength on the certificate have to agree with the listing that links to it. A 10 mg listing carrying a 5 mg report is a mismatch whether or not anyone intended it.

    Fails onpage 10 mg / COA 5 mg

  7. 07

    It is current

    A certificate describes one sample on one day. If it was tested more than six months ago, it almost certainly describes a lot that has already sold through, not the one being shipped now.

    Fails ontested > 6 months ago

The boundary

What we do not do

Three limits, stated plainly. Vagueness about who did the work is the easiest way to be misread as a lab, and we are not one.

No laboratory operated
We own no bench, no instrument and no analyst. Nothing on this site is measured by us.
No testing commissioned
We do not pay for assays or order panels of our own. Every result we read was paid for and published by someone else.
No product touched
Nothing is bought, stored, shipped or resold here. We never hold a vial, so we never have a reason to move one.

That boundary is the feature. Reading a document a vendor has already published costs minutes, so the same seven gates can run across a whole catalogue, run again when a lot changes, and run again next quarter. Commissioning our own testing would buy a deeper look at one vial and a far shorter list.

No vendor pays us for a listing or a verdict. Where a listing links out, our revenue is affiliate commission — which means refusing one costs us money, and that is exactly the trade that makes the refusals worth anything.

Statuses

Three outcomes, and none of them is silent.

These three statuses apply to the vendor registry only — a demonstration dataset while the first real audits are verified.

  • Verified

    The certificate passed all seven checks: it loads, names a laboratory that confirms it, was issued to this vendor, names the lot, matches the product page, and is recent.

  • Under review

    A check failed or could not be completed, and the vendor has been asked to fix it. The record stays visible, with the open question stated.

  • Delisted

    The vendor could not fix it, or the certificate was withdrawn. The record is kept permanently, with the reason for removal.

A check we could not run, because a vendor's site blocked us, is recorded as not checked. It is never recorded as a failure.

See it applied in the registry, or check each vendor's certificate record.
